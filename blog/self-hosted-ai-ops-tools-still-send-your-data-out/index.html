'Self-hosted' AI ops tools often still send your data out

When a Kubernetes service falls over at night, the start of an incident usually goes the same way. Alerts fire from several places at once. Someone opens dashboards, reads logs, and tries to work out which of the red things is the cause and which are reacting to it. The fix is often small, and most of the effort goes into finding it.

AI SRE tools promise to help with exactly this, and many of them do it well. Most of them run as a hosted service, though. To help you, they need your logs, metrics and incident details sent to them over the internet.

The teams that get left out

For teams in regulated industries and the public sector, that's often where the conversation ends. Production logs are full of things that aren't allowed to leave: internal hostnames, customer identifiers, pieces of requests. Security review asks where the data goes, and a hosted tool can't give a good answer.

Some of these teams run clusters with no internet connection at all. A tool that needs to call home can't work there, however good it is.

These teams also tend to run many clusters, with strict change control and little tolerance for long outages. They'd benefit a lot from help on call, and today they mostly go without it.

Bringing the assistant to the data

KubeSREAI is building an AI SRE for the whole estate that runs inside your own environment. It starts with Kubernetes, running next to the clusters it looks after. It's designed so your logs, metrics and incident details stay in your network, without calling any external AI service.

It's built to install and run in air-gapped environments too, and you can verify each release before you install it. It runs on regular CPUs, so it fits on the kind of servers you already have, and on GPUs for faster answers.

One AI SRE for the whole estate

Kubernetes is where KubeSREAI starts. Real incidents cross layers: a pod fails because a server's disk filled up, or a database behind it stopped answering. The goal is a complete AI SRE for the whole estate, planned to work the same way: servers and databases next, then virtual machines and network devices, then CI/CD pipelines and infrastructure code. On databases and network devices, a person will always approve the change.

"Self-hosted" doesn't always mean self-contained

Many tools now offer a self-hosted or "bring your own cloud" option. Often that means a collector runs in your environment, but the AI reasoning still happens on someone else's servers, so your logs and incident details still travel to reach it. If keeping data at home is the reason you're looking, these questions get to the answer quickly:

  1. Where does the AI model run? Inside your network, or behind an API somewhere else?
  2. What leaves your network during an incident? Not just metrics: prompts, log lines and summaries count too.
  3. Does it still work with all outbound traffic blocked? If not, it isn't usable in an isolated cluster.
  4. Can you check a release before installing it, without calling home?
  5. Who can change production? Can every change be held until a person approves it, and does it check that the fix worked?

KubeSREAI is designed to give the right answer to each of these: your data stays in your network, and nothing changes until a person approves it.

What it does during an incident

When something breaks, KubeSREAI works out what actually went wrong. Often the loudest alert is a symptom, such as a front end returning errors because a service behind it has failed. KubeSREAI traces it back to the service that broke and explains its reasoning in plain language.

It also draws on your own runbooks and postmortems, so its suggestions follow how your team already handles these problems.

Then it proposes a fix. Nothing changes in your cluster until a person approves it. Once a fix is applied, KubeSREAI checks whether it worked, and if it didn't, it hands the incident back to a person.

Many clusters, one place to look

Most organizations run more than one cluster. KubeSREAI gives you one console for all of them, so the on-call engineer sees every incident across the fleet in one place, while each cluster's data stays in its own environment.
